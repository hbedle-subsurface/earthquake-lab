# Find the Earthquake

An interactive seismology lab for introductory students, set in the Pacific Northwest above the Cascadia subduction zone.

I built this lab for my online gen ed course on volcanoes and earthquakes at the University of Oklahoma, where most students are not science majors and are meeting seismology for the first time. The idea is to learn by doing: each student gets their own synthetic earthquake, reads the seismograms from three stations, and works it the same way seismologists located earthquakes for most of the last century.

**Open the lab:** https://hbedle-subsurface.github.io/earthquake-lab/

## What students do

1. **Two waves** - move a station away from an earthquake and watch the S-P time grow.
2. **Read the seismograms** - mark the P and S arrivals at three stations.
3. **Time to distance** - turn each S-P time into a distance with travel-time curves.
4. **Find the epicenter** - draw three circles on a map of Washington and Oregon and triangulate.
5. **How big?** - measure amplitudes and read the magnitude off a Richter nomogram.
6. **What kind?** - place the hypocenter on a cross section through Cascadia: shallow crustal, megathrust or deep intraslab.
7. **Seconds of warning** - see how earthquake early warning (ShakeAlert) uses the gap between P and S waves.
8. **How big is big?** - compare the energy of their earthquake with Pawnee, Oklahoma (2016), Nisqually (2001) and the Cascadia earthquake of 1700.

Every step has something to move or tap, checks answers as students go, and has plain-language hints. When all eight steps are done, the lab makes a certificate with a verification code that students upload to the course site. Most students need about two hours.

Words with a dotted underline open a short glossary entry. The interactive panel and the instructions can each be popped out into their own window.

## A note on the science

The seismograms are synthetic, made from simple wave packets with P and S speeds of 6.0 and 3.5 km/s. The map, the trench line and the slab geometry are simplified for teaching and are not research models. Magnitude uses Richter's (1935) local magnitude relation in the nomogram form popularized by Bolt. Today most earthquakes are reported with moment magnitude, and the lab says so. The style of the exercise follows the long tradition of virtual earthquake labs, especially Gary Novak's *Virtual Earthquake* at California State University, Los Angeles.
